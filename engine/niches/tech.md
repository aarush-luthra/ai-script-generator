# Tech

## Who's watching
Everyday users of phones, laptops and apps, not engineers. They want to fix an annoyance, protect their accounts or get more from something they already own. They follow along with the phone in their other hand, so steps need to be exact and quick.

## What counts as a specific
Every item or beat gives the viewer at least one of these:
- the exact setting or feature by name, and the one menu it lives in ("it's under Privacy")
- what changes after they turn it on or off
- the reason it matters: the risk, the battery, the privacy, the time saved
- a concrete scenario where it helps or hurts
- which device or platform it applies to, when that matters

These are not specifics: "game-changer", "hidden feature", "hack", "life-changing", "you need this". If a line doesn't tell the viewer what to tap or why, cut it.

## Formats that work
- **Settings to change.** Each setting, what it saves the viewer from, then how to change it. All settings share one theme (privacy, battery, storage, interruptions) that the hook names; never mix themes to fill a count. At most 3 items at 30s, 4 at 45s, 5 at 60s.
- **Myth vs reality.** A common belief about devices, corrected.
- **Set it up once.** A short walk-through that prevents a future problem.
- **The mistake.** What most people do that costs them security, battery or time.
- **Head-to-head.** Two options, a clear verdict and the reason.

## Hooks that work here
- A myth with a cost: "Closing your apps isn't saving your battery."
- A risk the viewer hasn't thought of: "If someone steals your phone number, they can get into your email."
- A setup that pays off later: "Ten minutes today stops you getting locked out of everything."
- A setting with a stake: "One setting decides who can see where you took every photo."

## Banned phrases
- hack
- life hack
- hidden feature
- you need this
- life-changing
- tech tip

## Pitfalls
- Reading out menu paths. "Settings, Privacy and Security, Tracking" is robotic out loud, and viewers can pause. Name the setting and the one menu it's under, then spend the words on why it matters.
- Manual-speak: a run of bare commands ("Turn off X. Switch off Y."). Lead each step with the reason, then the action.
- Mixing themes to hit a number: a battery setting in a privacy video breaks the hook's promise.
- Features that differ by platform, presented as universal. Say which platform it applies to.
- Scare tactics without a fix.
- Listing features without saying why the viewer should care.

## Closes that work
- "Save this and do it tonight."
- "Send this to whoever does tech support in your family."
- A question: "Which of these did you already have on?"

## Editor checks
- Could the viewer find each setting from its name and the one menu it's under? (Full tap-by-tap paths are not wanted; they're robotic out loud.)
- Does every setting serve the hook's one theme?
- Does every item say why it matters, not just what it is?
- Is any setting name or claim likely to be wrong or platform-specific without saying so?

## Example scripts
These show the level of detail to match. Never reuse their content or phrasing.

### Set it up once · 45s · conversational
**Hook:** Your email password is the one that unlocks everything else.

**Body:** Every "forgot password" link goes to your inbox. So if someone gets into your email, they can reset your bank, your socials, all of it. Start there. Give your email a long password you don't use anywhere else, ideally made by a password manager. Then turn on two-step verification, and pick an authenticator app instead of text messages. Texts can be stolen by someone who talks your carrier into moving your number to their SIM. Last, save the backup codes somewhere offline. That's ten minutes, and it protects every account behind that inbox.

**CTA:** Send this to whoever does tech support in your family.

### Myth vs reality · 30s · blunt
**Hook:** Closing all your apps isn't saving your battery.

**Body:** Your phone already pauses apps you're not using. When you swipe them away, it has to load them from scratch next time, and that costs more power than leaving them. Both Apple and Google say you don't need to do it. If your battery's draining, check battery settings for the app that's actually using it.

**CTA:** Tell me how many apps you just stopped closing.
